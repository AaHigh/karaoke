Karaoke — Street Studio Design

The Civic is the microphone.

A Honda Civic wired as a four-channel microphone for street karaoke: SM57s on every corner, XLR running permanently into the car, and two phones sample-matched into one spatial recording. Sing live in mono today — and the next step is video before stereo: a real camera on the iPhone through the Accsoon SeeMo Pro, so YouTube Live looks as good as the street sounds, and someone at home in headphones feels like they are standing right here.

01

Why a car, why four channels?

Bars mean drunk crowds, reflections, and background noise. The street is controllable: your own space, your own pace, and room to dial in the technicals. The car stops being transport and becomes the instrument.

Spatial presence, not just loudness

A singer does not stand in a vacuum. Four corner mics capture where things are — voice, speaker, street, theatre crowd — relative to the car. Played back in headphones, that position information is what makes a listener feel present.

Three stereo layers, one field

The finished sound stacks three things: 1) the car’s spatial capture, 2) the true stereo field of the music track, and 3) the stereo field the Boss VE-20 generates for the voice. Each layer keeps its own width.

Built for the back end of the mix

The iRig Pro Duo sits after the SOUNDBOKS mix, not in the singer’s monitor path. No low-latency monitoring to depend on — just clean, high-quality digital recording of a mix that is already made.

02

The car as a four-channel microphone

The XLR pass-throughs are their own independent module — designed in now, installed last. Once they are in, setup is: put the mics on the corners, plug in, sing. Tap a corner to see its job.

Four corners, four channels

Each SM57 is an instrument mic — tough, directional, and happy outdoors. Together they turn the footprint of the car into a recording array you can mix like a 3D environment.

WIRING RULE — Sealed XLR pass-throughs from interior to exterior, with no sound leakage and the same polarity on every channel. No windows left cracked open — that compromise ruins the isolation. Polarity discipline is what keeps the spatial image honest when the four channels are combined.

Two interfaces, two phones, one take

One iRig Pro Duo takes two channels; the design uses two iRig Pro Duo interfaces and two phones recording simultaneously — four channels total, captured at the same moment, on the same performance.

Afterwards the two stereo recordings are sample-matched — aligned sample-for-sample — and combined into a single four-channel file. No re-takes, no drifting clocks in the finished mix.

The pass-through is a module — and it goes in last

The XLR run from interior to exterior is an independent module in this design, deliberately scheduled last. Running cable through the dash is car work, not audio work — a professional car stereo installer is the likely call, someone experienced with dashes and factory stereo removal, so nothing in the car gets messed up.

The requirement is simple and non-negotiable: sealed, with no sound leakage. No windows left cracked open to sneak a cable through — that is too compromised, and it ruins the isolation the whole array depends on.

A mixer’s view of the street

With four discrete corner channels, balance becomes composition: bring the front pair up for the singer and speaker, keep the rear pair low for street depth, or open all four when a crowd gathers.

  • Front pair — the performance, close and direct
  • Rear pair — the place: traffic, voices, the District at night
  • The blend — dialled per song, per night, by ear
03

Video first: the SeeMo puts a real camera on the phone

The video plan now comes before the stereo second speaker. The cameras on hand are too good to sit unused — and the return on this rig is online, where a viewer drops in to experience another place, acoustically and visually. No computer in the car: the phones are the studio.

First, keep two words straight: SeeMo ≠ CMOS

They sound alike, and dictation kept swapping them — so here is the distinction, written down:

  • SeeMo — S-E-E-M-O — is the Accsoon adapter: the hardware that plugs a camera into the iPhone.
  • CMOS is the image sensor inside the cameras — the Sony camcorders below run on an Exmor R CMOS sensor.

When this page says SeeMo, it means the adapter. When it says CMOS, it means the sensor.

The street camera: Sony HDR-CX290

Several Sony HDR-CX290 Handycams are already on hand and unused — Aaron says “CP290” for the same camera. Each has micro-HDMI out and an Exmor R CMOS sensor. One of them becomes the street camera, feeding the SeeMo, so the live picture comes from a real camcorder instead of the phone’s own lens.

The car’s own eyes are compact SDI cameras on 12-volt power, with a screw-in lens bought separately for each. They mount up high — above the windshield and above the back window — centered with the corner mics, and relatively concealed: part of the car, not gadgets stuck on it.

SDI end to end — no converters

Staying SDI the whole way saves the Blackmagic hardware that would otherwise convert back and forth to HDMI. The chain has no converters in between: cameras straight into the SeeMo Pro, SeeMo Pro into the phone, phone out to YouTube.

The VE-20 already thinks in stereo

The Boss VE-20 has stereo output — so the voice does not have to wait for Phase C to get width. Take the VE-20’s right channel and feed it alongside the SOUNDBOKS mono mix, and the voice opens into its own stereo field while the YouTube track stays mono underneath it.

That is the bridge: mono music, stereo voice, today — true stereo everything when the Mix arrives.

Why not just wire the stereo out?

A wired adapter off the playback device could hand the iRig a stereo feed directly — worth testing. But the long-term answer is still the paired boxes: the way SOUNDBOKS designs TeamUp, left and right off two matched speakers is simply the cleaner, louder, weatherproof path for a street rig.

06

Live, on camera, and powered all night

A recording rig that dies at 9 p.m. or films the singer’s back is not a studio. These systems close those gaps.

Stereo YouTube Live — from the phone

YouTube Live carries stereo — so busking goes out the way it sounds on the street. A listener at home in headphones gets the spatial presence of the car, the true stereo music, and the VE-20’s stereo voice: like being here.

The channel stands at 1,099 subscribers — past the 1,000 YouTube requires to go live from a phone. That milestone is already earned, so YouTube Live from the phone is the stereo path, fed by the SeeMo Pro and the free Accsoon SEE app at up to 1080p60.

TikTok Live — later, and it needs a computer

TikTok Live in stereo is not a phone job: it takes a computer running OBS or TikTok Live Studio. This rig is deliberately phone-first — no computer in the car — so TikTok Live stereo comes later, when a computer joins the setup. YouTube carries the live stereo audience for now.

Camera-tracking tripod

The phone rides a camera-tracking tripod and follows the singer as they move. No static wide shot, no cameraman needed — the frame stays on the performance while both hands stay on the music.

Front & rear SDI cameras

The corner mics hear the whole car; the cameras see it from both ends. Compact 12-volt SDI cameras — screw-in lens bought separately — sit up high above the windshield and above the back window, centered with the corner mics, relatively concealed, and feed straight into the SeeMo Pro. One of the Sony HDR-CX290 camcorders covers the street view.

Power: car + 100 Ah LiFePO₄

Two reservoirs: the car battery (with the alternator run intermittently — which also keeps the car warm on cold nights) and the 100 Ah LiFePO₄ in the back seat. USB-C adapters and PD splitters power the iRig Pro Duo and charge the iPhone 16 Pro at the same time.

Open item: the stereo mix into SDI

On the SeeMo path, audio rides embedded in the SDI signal into the stream. Getting the iRig stereo mix embedded into that SDI feed is still an open item — noted here so it is solved deliberately, not discovered live on the street.

POWER NOTE — The iRig Pro Duo can also run directly off the iPhone 16 Pro for a quick test run, for as long as the phone battery supports it. The full power system is what turns a test into a whole evening.

Rollout — free first

Get them hooked, then talk about money.

In the short term, sessions are free. A professional musician who walks up in the Tower District, sings into a real stereo rig, and hears a studio-level spatial recording of themselves does not need a sales pitch — they need a second song.

Monetization comes later, once the rig is proven and the musicians already know what it does. The design comes first. The business follows the sound.
